# Cultural Public Sphere Analysis

## Theoretical orientation
Treat social media as a mediated arena in which cultural expression, affect, identity, representation, and civic discourse may intersect with public issues. Do not assume every online conversation is a deliberative political public sphere. Make the theoretical choice explicit and justify it.

Use the cultural public sphere as a sensitizing framework, not a label imposed on data. Where useful, organize interpretation around:
- **Structural dimension:** access, inclusivity/exclusion, platform affordances, participation conditions.
- **Representational dimension:** actors, collective identities, constituencies, visibility, and who speaks for whom.
- **Interactional dimension:** discourse, symbols, affect, reflexivity, contestation, and meaning-making.

## Research workflow
1. Define issue, place, platform, time window, hashtag/keyword, policy domain, and research questions.
2. Reconstruct context: policy timeline, official decisions, local history, relevant institutions, and offline events. Separate verified events from participants' claims.
3. Establish corpus provenance and sampling boundaries. Document query syntax, collection date, platform API/export limits, deleted/unavailable posts, and language coverage.
4. Map participation: volume, active periods, account types where supportable, interaction patterns, and barriers to visibility.
5. Identify communities using network analysis only when edge data and graph construction are defensible. Describe communities as algorithmic clusters, not naturally bounded social groups.
6. Model topics or code themes to locate recurring cultural frames, concerns, symbols, emotions, and policy references.
7. Conduct close discourse analysis on purposively selected posts/threads. Explain selection criteria, context, coding dimensions, and translation decisions.
8. Triangulate digital traces with policy documents, news, interviews, public statements, or observable offline actions where available.
9. Evaluate whether and how online discourse enters agenda-setting or policy evaluation. Trace mechanisms and chronology; do not infer influence from temporal coincidence alone.

## Analytical questions
- Who can participate and whose voices are absent?
- Which cultural symbols, places, memories, identities, and emotions organize the discussion?
- Which actors and communities become visible, and how are they connected?
- How are claims framed, challenged, translated, amplified, or ignored?
- What links online expression to formal policy processes or offline collective action?
- Does engagement lead to demonstrable action, or is evidence limited to symbolic participation?

## Evidence discipline
For each claim, classify evidence as:
- Direct observation (post, official document, timestamp, network edge)
- Analytical inference (theme, frame, community role)
- Contextual corroboration (interview, news, archive, policy record)
- Unverified participant assertion

Never treat platform activity as a proxy for the entire local population. Discuss digital divide, self-selection, algorithmic visibility, moderation, and sampling bias. Avoid calling a movement inclusive or representative without evidence about who is missing.

## Deliverable
Produce a narrative with: theoretical framing; case context; corpus and sampling; structural, representational, and interactional findings; discourse excerpts anonymized where appropriate; policy relevance; triangulation; limitations; and references.

## Reference anchor
Fahmi (2019), "Cultural Public Sphere: Tracking the Yogyakarta City Policy Agenda through the #JogjaOraDidol Hashtag on Twitter", DOI: 10.30589/pgr.v3i1.123. The published study combines topic modelling, social network analysis, and discourse analysis. Use it as a methodological precedent, not as a universal template.
